01 / The Problem

Visual breaks where the user actually trains.

Most amateur cricket and badminton happens in nets. Indoor nets, outdoor nets, mixed-lighting nets, single-camera handheld phone-on-tripod nets. This is where the user shoots their training video — and this is exactly where the visual-only event-detection pipeline starts to fall apart.

In broadcast footage, the ball-on-bat contact frame is unambiguous: clean light, stable camera, 4K resolution, the ball is always visible. In nets footage, the same event is partially occluded by the net itself, partially clipped by the camera framing, partially blurred by 30 FPS motion, partially happening behind the batter's body. The visual pipeline reports either no detection (model didn't see it) or false detection (model saw the ball pass and assumed contact). Either way: analytics downstream gets corrupted.

The audio modality is invariant to all of this. The contact sound is the contact sound — same acoustic signature regardless of camera angle, lighting, net density, or motion blur. If we can train a model to classify the bat-ball contact sound vs ambient noise, we have an event detector that works in the same place the user actually generates the footage.

02 / The Modality Choice

Why audio, not better video.

The alternative would have been investing more in visual robustness — better occlusion handling, motion-blur compensation, multi-frame temporal smoothing. We chose audio. Here's the comparison that drove the decision.

Visual

Visual contact detection · what breaks

The visual pipeline that powers shuttle tracking and cricket ball tracking also tries to detect bat-ball contact. In broadcast footage it's fine. In nets footage it isn't.

  • Net mesh partially occludes the contact frame
  • 30 FPS motion blur smears the ball at contact moment
  • Single-angle phone captures lose contact behind the batter
  • Mixed indoor lighting creates inconsistent ball-on-bat texture
  • Same model that ships 93% on broadcast badminton drops to unusable in nets
  • Improving any of these is months of training data, not weeks
Audio

Audio contact detection · what survives

The acoustic signature of bat-ball contact is modality-invariant. The microphone hears the contact sound whether or not the camera saw it.

  • Net mesh doesn't block sound
  • Frame-rate irrelevant — audio sample rate is 44.1 kHz
  • Camera angle doesn't matter — phone mic is omnidirectional
  • Lighting irrelevant
  • Same model serves broadcast AND nets footage
  • 106 labelled samples sufficient for first viable model

03 / The Iteration

68% to 86.36% in 23 hours.

The build cycle spanned 9 days end-to-end (Jun 19–27, 2025), but the actual accuracy jump happened in a single overnight cycle — after an annotation-export bug was identified and fixed.

First training run on 106 samples hit 68% — below target, and the LabelStudio JSON export bug had just been resolved. After fine-tuning, the next cycle hit 86.36% the following morning. The September 2025 production deployment on an expanded dataset settled at ~85%, stable.
04 / The Bug

The annotation that looked correct in the UI.

— Linear ticket AI-68 · June 26, 2025 · 09:34 UTC

I have annotated the clips twice, but when exporting in JSON format, every annotation is marked as miss.

yash.v@khiladipro.com · Linear AI-68 comment

This is the kind of bug that doesn't break anything visibly. The LabelStudio UI showed both hit and miss annotations correctly. The training pipeline accepted the exported JSON without error. But the exported labels were corrupted — every annotation, whether marked hit or miss in the UI, exported as "miss." A model trained on that data would learn to predict "miss" every time, regardless of the actual audio.

The bug was in the LabelStudio JSON export configuration — a templating issue where the hit-label keyword wasn't being written to the exported file. The fix took less than an hour once identified. Finding it took longer.

Why this matters for the portfolio: the lesson isn't "labelling tools have bugs." The lesson is silent data corruption is the most dangerous bug class in ML pipelines. If the bug had survived undetected, every downstream training cycle would have been chasing model improvements against corrupted ground truth — a model that "improves" without ever working. The willingness to annotate twice and notice the discrepancy, dig into the export pipeline, and find the real story is what unlocked the 86.36% the next morning. Most ML projects die quietly to silent data corruption. This one didn't.

By 13:35 UTC the same day, the training pipeline was re-run on corrected labels: Total samples loaded: 106, Model Accuracy: 0.68. By 13:37 UTC: "The accuracy is 68% I will work on it further to improve the accuracy." By 08:42 UTC the next morning (Jun 27): "Improved Model Accuracy: 86.36%".

07 / Honest Limitations

What's actually in production.

Every system has limitations. Pretending the audio detector is bulletproof would be dishonest — and would set up downstream consumers for failures they couldn't anticipate.

— 01

No per-class breakdown reported

The 85% production accuracy is overall. Hit vs miss class-level accuracy isn't separately reported — if one class is consistently harder than the other (likely miss, since "miss" includes more acoustic variation), the headline number hides it. V2 work would surface the per-class confusion matrix.
— 02

Microphone dependency

The model trains on audio from the same camera-microphone setup that captures the video. Different microphones produce different acoustic signatures. If the user switches phones, the audio characteristics change — model robustness across microphone hardware isn't formally benchmarked.
— 03

Background noise floor

Works in quiet nets. Works in moderately-noisy nets. In very noisy environments — multiple courts active simultaneously, crowd noise, ambient music — the contact signature gets buried. Hard noise threshold beyond which the model degrades is not characterized.
— 04

Single-modality decision boundary

Audio is a binary classifier — hit or miss, no confidence calibration. No ensemble with visual for ambiguous cases. A model that says "I'm 51% confident this is a hit" gets treated the same as "99% confident." V2 should add confidence calibration and visual ensemble for low-confidence cases.
— 05

Cross-sport unproven

Trained and validated on cricket bat-ball contact. Whether the same architecture works on badminton shuttle-racket contact or other sport-specific acoustic events is not yet tested. The categorical claim about "noisy environments" is sport-specific evidence, not general evidence.
— 06

Latency not benchmarked

Production deploys batched (audio clip → label). Real-time / streaming-mode inference latency isn't measured. For analytics this is fine; for in-game overlays or real-time feedback, the latency profile would need to be characterized.
— 07

Held-out methodology not fully formal

The 85% comes from production evaluation but the held-out split methodology — same conditions vs cross-condition splits — isn't as formally documented as the shuttle benchmark's held-out clips + held-out venues structure. Same standard should apply here.
— 08

No IAA on the audio labels

Audio annotation is harder than visual — humans listening to ambiguous "did that bat catch the ball?" sounds disagree at non-trivial rates. Inter-annotator agreement was not formally measured. The model's accuracy ceiling is bounded by labelling noise we haven't characterized.
08 / What's Next

V2 priorities.

  • → 01Per-class confusion matrix. Decompose the 85% overall into hit-accuracy and miss-accuracy separately. The class that's consistently harder reveals where retraining effort should go.
  • → 02Cross-microphone robustness benchmark. Evaluate the model on audio from at least 3 different phone microphone profiles. If accuracy varies by >5pp across hardware, add microphone-aware preprocessing.
  • → 03Confidence calibration. The current model outputs binary labels. Add a confidence score calibrated against held-out accuracy — so "0.95 confidence" actually means 95% expected correctness. Enables downstream routing for low-confidence ambiguous events.
  • → 04Audio + visual ensemble for low-confidence cases. When audio confidence is borderline (e.g., 0.55-0.70), fall back to visual to break the tie. Pure audio when confident; ensemble when uncertain.
  • → 05Cross-sport extension to badminton. Shuttle-racket contact has a different acoustic signature but similar event-detection topology. Test whether the architecture transfers — if yes, one event-detection module serves multiple sports.
  • → 06Inter-annotator agreement (IAA) study. Re-annotate 10% of clips with a second labeller. The disagreement set bounds the labelling noise floor. If we're at 85% and human IAA is 87%, we're close to ceiling and need different evaluation, not more training.
